\subsection{Methodology}

All timings are the \textbf{median of 5 timed runs} preceded by one warmup
run, measured with warm OS page caches.
Our encoder processes individual string columns; DuckDB encodes the complete
file at once. Consequently, raw file sizes reported for DuckDB include all
columns (including numeric fields), whereas raw sizes for our system cover
only the tested string columns. Random access is not natively supported
as a point-query primitive in DuckDB over CSV/Parquet and is therefore
reported for our system only.

\section{Discussion}
% =============================================================================

\paragraph{Compression ratio.}
Adaptive compression excels on low-cardinality repeated columns:
\texttt{views\_stats} col.\,4 (project ID) achieves 45.79$\times$, while
DuckDB/Zstd reaches 24.49$\times$ on the same file. Per the paper's
specification, pages where more than 80\% of values are distinct bypass
dictionary encoding entirely and are stored raw. For fully unique columns
(\texttt{artifacts} cols.\,1--2), this reduces expansion from the previous
0.88--0.94$\times$ to 0.92--0.96$\times$; the residual overhead is the
unavoidable 4-byte length prefix per string in our binary format. The
\texttt{artifact\_authors} display name (col.\,3) is partially affected:
40\% of its pages are raw, yielding 0.88$\times$ — slightly worse than
the old local-dict encoding for that column because pages in the
80--93\% distinct range store duplicate values twice in raw mode whereas
a local dictionary would deduplicate them. DuckDB/Parquet with Zstd
remains stronger on free-text columns overall.

\paragraph{Full scan.}
DuckDB on Parquet is faster for full-column materialisation due to
vectorised execution and columnar projection. Raw-page decompression in
our system is notably faster than local-dict decompression: \texttt{artifacts}
cols.\,1--2 improved from 348--841\,ms to 207--442\,ms because raw pages
require only a direct value copy with no offset lookups. For
\texttt{views\_stats} (98.7\% diff pages) our decompressor reaches up to
1.06\,GB/s per column.

\paragraph{Filtered scan.}
On low-cardinality columns, our filtered scan is \textbf{competitive with
or faster than DuckDB/Parquet}. For \texttt{views\_stats} col.\,1 (month)
we achieve 2.1--3.0\,ms versus 3.7--3.9\,ms for Parquet/Snappy. Raw pages
introduce a trade-off: they cannot use dict-miss skipping, so fully unique
columns (\texttt{artifacts} col.\,1) now scan all 58 pages linearly
($\approx$17.6\,ms) compared to near-instant scans when local dicts
allowed 57-of-58 pages to be dict-skipped. Similarly, the
\texttt{artifact\_authors} display name now dict-skips only 6--62 of 152
pages (down from 27--106) because raw pages are opaque to the scanner,
raising scan times from 1.4--3.3\,ms to 9.3--11.8\,ms --- still well
below DuckDB/Parquet's 104--109\,ms. On fully low-cardinality columns
(access type, boolean, type), both systems must scan every page and
DuckDB/Parquet wins due to vectorised filtering (3--7\,ms vs.\ 4--11\,ms).

\paragraph{Random access.}
Our implementation provides sub-2\,\textmu{}s point queries via the
two-page read scheme. Raw pages resolve in a single direct index with no
dictionary lookup, keeping latency at the same sub-microsecond level.
This capability is not directly available in DuckDB over CSV/Parquet
without a full table scan or pre-built index.
